# Fairness audit — retention model

Updated 2026-11-01. Positive class is **will_return** (a purchase within 90 days), not churn. The tables below are written by `python -m src.fairness`; its raw output is `reports/current_run/fairness_audit.json`. The findings quote numbers from those tables; re-read them after any retraining.

## What was measured

- **Model and data.** The saved model (checksum verified) on the untouched test snapshot, at the stored decision threshold. The audit recomputes every test probability and stops if it differs from `artifacts/test_predictions.parquet`.
- **Groups, as the brief asks (section 3.7).**
  - *Region*: the customer's majority country, grouped as United Kingdom, Other Europe, Rest of world and Unknown.
  - *Tenure band*: days since first purchase, in four bands: under 6 months, 6–12 months, 12–18 months, and 18 months or more.
  - *Spend quartile*: cumulative spend at the cut-off, split into quarters of the audited customers.
  - *Purchase history*: one-time versus repeat buyers. It is not in the brief's list; it is added because the earlier error analysis found a large gap there.
- **Per group.** Rows, returners and return rate. Recall of returners, with a Wilson 95% interval. Precision. And selection rate: the share scored at or above the threshold, which is the share the assumed coupon policy would select.
- **Flag.** A group is flagged when its recall is more than 5 percentage points below overall recall (the brief's rule).
  - *Strong*: the whole 95% interval stays below that line.
  - *Inconclusive*: the group is small enough that its interval still reaches the line.

These groups are proxies built from purchase behaviour and country. The data has no protected characteristics such as age, sex or ethnicity. So this audit cannot show demographic fairness, and having no demographic inputs does not rule out indirect discrimination.

## Findings

1. **No region is flagged.** Other Europe's recall is a little above the United Kingdom's. Rest of world and Unknown are too small to support any conclusion: their 95% intervals span most of the possible range.
2. **Every flag describes customers with a thin purchase history.** The flagged groups are one-time buyers, the two lowest spend quartiles, and customers under 12 months of tenure. All flags are strong, not small-sample effects. These groups are largely the same people (see the overlap line under the tables):
   - most of the lowest spend quartile are one-time buyers;
   - together, the flagged groups cover most of the test snapshot.
3. **Why.** The model ranks customers mainly on their purchase history (recency, frequency, spend). Customers with little history get low calibrated probabilities. Partly this is accurate: they really do return less often. The lowest spend quartile's return rate is a fraction of the highest's. But one shared threshold then catches far fewer of the ones who do return. With different base rates, a calibrated model cannot also give every group the same recall at a single threshold. Closing the gap means choosing what to give up.
4. **Business consequence.** The assumed policy sends a coupon to customers scored at or above the threshold. It would select almost all of the highest spend quartile and very few of the lowest. That policy targets customers likely to return anyway, not customers a coupon might persuade. This finding is one more reason the placeholder costs and the targeting goal need a business decision (`configs/pipeline.yaml`).

## Candidate mitigations and trade-offs

None is implemented. Choosing one is a business and ethics decision.

1. **Lower the threshold for flagged groups.** The last table measures this on the June 2011 decision period, not the test snapshot, so the holdout is not tuned on.
   - *Gain*: it closes each recall gap.
   - *Cost*: for the lowest spend quartile and for one-time buyers, the threshold falls to about half. Most of the group is then selected, and precision roughly halves, so more coupons go to customers who will not return.
   - *Ethics*: it also treats customers differently by group. That needs a written justification and a review of how it would be explained to customers (Law 25 transparency).
2. **Give thin-history customers better features, or a model of their own.** Candidates: the first basket's value, size and product mix, how the first purchase came about, and seasonality.
   - *Gain*: it could raise recall without selecting everyone.
   - *Cost*: research, and a new holdout period for honest evaluation. The available data may simply carry little signal for these customers.
3. **Change the objective from "likely to return" to "likely to be persuaded".** This means uplift modelling.
   - *Gain*: coupons would go where they change behaviour, which is also the fairer question to ask.
   - *Cost*: it needs a randomized coupon experiment. This dataset cannot estimate uplift at all.
4. **Send borderline scores in flagged groups to human review.**
   - *Gain*: the gap can be judged case by case.
   - *Cost*: staff time, and reviewers need written guidance so decisions stay consistent.
5. **Reweight training toward thin-history customers.**
   - *Gain*: it is cheap to try.
   - *Cost*: it may lower overall PR-AUC and calibration, so it needs revalidation on the decision period before any test evaluation.

## What this audit does not cover

- **No protected characteristics.** The groups are behavioural and geographic proxies only.
- **No per-group calibration.** Calibration by group, intersectional groups and correction for multiple comparisons are not reported.
- **One holdout snapshot.** Results come from a single snapshot (cut-off 2011-09-01). Customers recur across snapshots, so this measures known customers, not new ones.
- **Mitigation estimates are not test results.** They come from development data and are estimates, not tested outcomes.
